## Before you cold-mail me about positions in my lab, please read this...

As academics we get many "cold" queries from people looking to work with us, either on a voluntary basis, as a PhD student, or perhaps as a post-doc. With the advent of AI, the number of these queries have ballooned to the extent that I can no longer respond individually to each message. Therefore, I have crafted an initial default response that clarifies my position. 

---

Thank you for your message and your interest in my research. I am very happy to talk with students who demonstrate a genuine interest in my work and whose background and skillset meshes well with what I do. However, I get a *lot* of these messages and their frequency only seems to increase since the advent of AI. As a result, I can no longer respond to each sender individually. Therefore, I apologise for sending an initial blanket response. The aim of this response is to assess to what extent your interest is genuine, and also to answer some common questions that you may have.

If your interest is genuine and our research interests really are a good match and the information provided below does not fully address any questions you might have, then please do respond and I am very happy to engage.

First, let me clarify my position with regard to relatively generic e-mails (often clearly generated with the help of AI).

- **If you have used AI in crafting your initial contact e-mail, you are unlikely to get a positive response.** I appreciate that if English is not your first language, AI can be tremendously useful in translating and improving a message you have drafted in your native language. Nevertheless, I do need to know whether you can express yourself in English. Otherwise we cannot work together. I don't mind if your message is not grammatically perfect or free from spelling mistakes, provided the text makes sense and is properly tailored to the recipient (i.e. me). Regardless of what your first language is, if you have used AI actually to create the content of your message (i.e. going beyond just translating and spelling/grammar correction), it is impossible for me to know *your* interests are, and what and how *you* think. 
- I appreciate things are tough out there. In order to get research experience or a PhD or post-doc position, you may have to contact many people/labs. I appreciate that this must be enormously time-consuming and a "scatter gun" approach can therefore be tempting. However, it is quite easy to spot such messages. They will typically say something along the lines of "I am particularly interested in how... `<insert words from my website or a mash-up of titles from some of my papers>`". Academics are quite vain: we like people liking what we do. But if we sense that a message was probably sent (with some modifications) to everyone and their dog, we are unlikely to answer. Actually, I find it very difficult not to answer; hence this message! 
- Therefore, you are better off approaching a small number of people in whose work you are genuinely interested and for which you are genuinely well suited. So rather than spending your time sending out lots of e-mails, spend that time engaging with someone's work and articulating why and how that fits with your interests and experience. This process can actually be enormously helpful for yourself to work out what it is that you are interested in and would like to pursue. The message does not have to be long, but you want to show that you have genuinely considered the match.

Second, let me comment about the availability of positions and funding in my lab.

- The funding climate is really tough. **PhD funding is very limited, especially for overseas students.** As a School, I guess we have access to only about 4-8 studentship opportunities a year. Often these studentships have certain conditions/restrictions on them that mean that they are most likely out of reach for me (e.g. targeted at specific research priority areas for the university). In any event, the limited studentships are distributed over (rough estimate) ~80 academics (but often more, when the competition goes beyond the school). You can see that the chances of getting a funded PhD position are very slim. So the candidate + supervisor(s) + project proposal "package" has to be extremely strong to be competitive. When there are opportunities to compete for studentships, these will be advertised on websites such as [findaphd.com](https://www.findaphd.com/){:target="_blank"} and [jobs.ac.uk](https://www.jobs.ac.uk/){:target="_blank"}.
- Getting PhD funding through project grants held by a Principal Investigator (PI, i.e. someone like me) is no longer really possible in the UK, so that route to PhD funding is pretty much closed off. But again, should such funding become available (e.g. through grants from elsewhere), then these opportunities will be advertised on [findaphd.com](https://www.findaphd.com/){:target="_blank"} and [jobs.ac.uk](https://www.jobs.ac.uk/){:target="_blank"}, and [elsewhere on these pages](https://casludwig.github.io/Current-opportunities/). 
- Unfortunately, as I mentioned, for overseas students the situation is even more difficult. However, some countries have their own competitive studentship schemes (e.g. Chinese Scholarship Council) that allow people to go abroad for further study. You would have to apply for the grant yourself, but I would be happy to support and help you with your application *if* our research interests mesh well and we have landed on a good idea to pursue.
- One option is to self-fund your PhD. I personally feel very ambivalent about this. Principally, I feel that doing research is work and should be recognised and remunerated as such. Moreover, in many cases, I am not convinced that for the student's longer-term life/career goals, the PhD will make a major difference, which makes it hard to justify the expense (although that is obviously hard for me to judge). Then again, sometimes people have saved up or work part-time and they simply want to spend some of their time doing interesting stuff and learning/creating new knowledge. In these instances, I feel more inclined to support their application.
- I have very limited capacity to supervise students wanting to work on a voluntary basis in my lab, perhaps as a stepping stone to a (funded) PhD position here in Bristol or elsewhere. Again, I feel quite ambivalent about this, for the same reason as above (research is work), but also because I'm conscious that volunteering is not a route that is available to everyone. 
- For post-doc opportunities, these are almost exclusively tied to grants awarded to the PI or to the candidate themselves. If I have funding for post-docs, opportunities will be advertised through  [jobs.ac.uk](https://www.jobs.ac.uk/){:target="_blank"}, the [University's vacancies portal](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/jobs/){:target="_blank"} and [elsewhere on these pages](https://casludwig.github.io/Current-opportunities/). If you have acquired funding yourself to go somewhere and you've decided that somewhere is my lab at the University of Bristol, then great - let's talk. Or if you are thinking of applying for funding to come and work in my lab, then again I am happy to support and help you with your application *if* our research interests mesh well and we have landed on a good idea to pursue.
